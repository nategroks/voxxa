# Human-AI shared control, self-training loops, volunteer onboarding and live-event reliability for an autonomous slide follower

Scope: HCI / automation literature (arXiv cs.HC, cs.AI, cs.SD plus classic sources), live-captioning and teleprompter practice, church-tech practitioner sources, and theatre/broadcast show-control practice, 2018 to Nov 2026 (with pre-2018 classics where they are the canonical source). Context for relevance judgments only: a Windows app that listens to the lead singer and presses "next slide" in the presentation app, with a practice-session recorder (audio + tapped true slide changes), a live helper with dry-run mode, and a hard rule that it never presses keys unless the presentation app is the front window.

## Key Question 1: What does HCI / automation literature say about handoff, override, trust calibration and "human on the loop" for semi-autonomous systems run by non-experts?

### Takeaway
The classic literature converges on a few rules that map directly onto a slide follower: pick the automation level per *stage* (sense / analyse / decide / act) rather than globally; calibrate trust by showing how well the system can do (confidence) rather than just what it did; keep override cheap and global; and avoid "automation surprise" by making the system's next action predictable and observable before it fires. Confidence display helps calibrate trust but does not by itself improve joint accuracy; the human has to be able to contribute something the system lacks (e.g., knowing the worship leader repeated the chorus).

### Cited Findings

**Levels and types of automation**
- Parasuraman, Sheridan & Wickens (2000) propose a four-stage model (information acquisition, information analysis, decision selection, action implementation) where "each type of automation can have different degrees or levels of automation (LOA), depending on the context"; it builds on Sheridan & Verplank's (1978) ten levels from fully manual to fully autonomous — [Parasuraman, Sheridan & Wickens 2000, IEEE SMC-A (DOI)](https://doi.org/10.1109/3468.844354); summarised in [Clemson CAT Lab, Pak et al. 2017](https://blogs.clemson.edu/catlab/files/2021/09/Pak-et-al.-2017-Does-the-domain-of-technology-impact-user-trust-Inve-...-consumer-oriented-domains-in-young-adults-military-and-older-adults.pdf) and [Cummings, Duke, IEEE Intelligent Systems expert opinion](https://hal.pratt.duke.edu/sites/hal.pratt.duke.edu/files/u10/IS-29-05-Expert%20Opinion[1]_0.pdf) ("at the lower levels the human is typically actively involved in the decision-making process").
- The EU-style "AI Assessment Catalog" (arXiv 2307.03681) formally distinguishes *human-in-the-loop* (the system "cannot finish a task without human confirmation"; the user can "correct, override and compensate") from *human-on-the-loop* (intervention "is not possible at all times, but humans can subsequently correct, override and compensate for decisions made automatically") — [arXiv 2307.03681](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.03681).
- FAA Human Factors Design Standard, Ch. 3 Automation: an automated system "should allow for manual override" — [FAA HFDS Ch3](https://hf.tc.faa.gov/hfds/download-hfds/hfds_pdfs/Ch3_Automation.pdf).

**Trust calibration**
- Lee & See (2004) is the seminal model of trust in automation; one bibliometric review (arXiv 2309.09828) reports that about one quarter of all empirical trust-in-AI articles cite it. Lee & See define automation as "technology that actively selects data, transforms information, makes decisions, or controls processes" and frame the goal as *appropriate reliance* (calibration), not maximum trust — [Lee & See 2004, Human Factors](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1518/hfes.46.1.50_30392); [arXiv 2309.09828 review](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.09828).
- Zhang, Liao & Bellamy (2020): showing the model's confidence score "helped calibrate people's trust in the AI", but better calibration "did not by itself improve joint human-AI decision accuracy"; improvement depended on whether the human could contribute knowledge complementary to the AI's errors. They also report problems with case-specific (local) explanations — [arXiv 2001.02114](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.02114).
- Akash et al. (2020): dynamically varying automation *transparency* based on a real-time model of human trust improved team performance versus a non-adaptive decision aid — [arXiv 2006.16353](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.16353v1).
- Li & Zhang (2023, v3 Nov 2025): a shared-autonomy strategy where the robot changes its autonomy level while preserving human trust, inferring trust from time-stamped interaction events (Bayesian Relational Event Modeling) — [arXiv 2311.02009](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.02009v1).
- Trust-aware assistance seeking under human-supervised autonomy with real-time trust estimation — [arXiv 2410.20496](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.20496v1).
- Warmsley et al. (Frontiers in Robotics & AI, 2025, not arXiv): a closed-loop system where the machine self-assesses capability, predicts human trust, and asks for help when appropriate reported roughly a 40% gain in human trust and about 5% in team performance versus baseline (unverified beyond the search summary) — [Frontiers 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/frobt.2025.1557075/full).
- Counter-intuitive result: Honeycutt, Nourani & Ragan (HCOMP 2020) found that letting users provide human-in-the-loop feedback *lowered* both their trust in the system and their perceived accuracy of it — [Honeycutt et al. HCOMP 2020](https://www.cise.ufl.edu/~eragan/papers/Honeycutt_HCOMP2020.pdf).

**Ironies of automation / out-of-the-loop / automation surprise**
- Bainbridge (1983) "Ironies of Automation": an operator who spends long periods only monitoring becomes de-skilled; vigilance research shows it is "impossible for even a highly motivated human being" to sustain reliable watchfulness over rare events, so monitoring for unlikely abnormalities "has to be done by an automatic alarm system"; operators therefore need *more* training for rare interventions, not less — [Bainbridge 1983 PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/sociology/technology/1983-bainbridge.pdf); [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ironies_of_Automation); [Adrian Colyer's summary](https://blog.acolyer.org/2020/01/08/ironies-of-automation/).
- An arXiv paper on applying the ironies to AI-assisted design (de-skilling, cognitive offloading, misplaced responsibility) — [arXiv 2503.03924](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.03924).
- Xu et al. (HCI for autonomous vehicles): systems that worked well under normal conditions caused operators "automation surprise" when unexpected events occurred, linked to an "out-of-the-loop" effect in which operators do not understand what the automation is doing and have low situation awareness (unverified: seen in search summary of the ResearchGate PDF) — [Xu, From Automation to Autonomy (ResearchGate PDF)](https://researchgate.net/profile/Wei-Xu-151/publication/340270485_From_Automation_to_Autonomy_and_Autonomous_Vehicles_Challenges_and_Opportunities_for_Human-Computer_Interaction/links/5ff900f245851553a02e7a96/From-Automation-to-Autonomy-and-Autonomous-Vehicles-Challenges-and-Opportunities-for-Human-Computer-Interaction.pdf); related: [Xu, Dainoff, Ge, Gao, arXiv 2105.05424](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.05424) (abstract names "seven main issues" for HCI with AI; specifics not in abstract).
- Classic (not fetched this session, flagged as such): Boer & Dekker (2017), "Models of Automation Surprise", Safety 3(3):20 — [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2313-576X/3/3/20); Klein, Woods, Bradshaw, Hoffman & Feltovich (2004), "Ten Challenges for Making Automation a 'Team Player'", IEEE Intelligent Systems — [IEEE](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/1363742). The latter is the usual source for *observability, predictability, directability* of automation.

**Design heuristics**
- Jalaeian, Morey & Rayo (Dec 2025): fourteen heuristics for human-machine joint activity synthesised from display design, human factors, cognitive systems engineering; "systems must always allow for user override", and separately, systems should include aids that help users "break away from the influence of automation when it is incorrect or misleading" (second clause from search snippet; abstract lists five macrocognitive functions: Event Detection, Sensemaking, Adaptability, Perspective-Shifting, Coordination) — [arXiv 2512.08036](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.08036).
- "Alert of the Second Decision-maker: An Introduction to Human-AI Conflict" — frames the case where human and AI disagree on an action and how conflict should be surfaced — [arXiv 2305.16477](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.16477).
- Amershi et al. (CHI 2019), 18 Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction, synthesised from 150+ recommendations: G1 "Make clear what the system can do"; G2 "Make clear how well the system can do what it can do"; G3 "Time services based on context"; G7-G9 "Support efficient invocation / dismissal / correction"; G10 "Scope services when in doubt"; G11 "Make clear why the system did what it did"; G13 "Learn from user behavior"; G14 "Update and adapt cautiously"; G15 "Encourage granular feedback"; G16 "Convey the consequences of user actions"; G17 "Provide global controls"; G18 "Notify users about changes" — [Microsoft Research blog](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/guidelines-for-human-ai-interaction-design/); [publication page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/guidelines-for-human-ai-interaction/). Framed as a 20th-anniversary follow-up to Horvitz's CHI 1999 mixed-initiative principles.

**Practitioner rule relevant to override design**
- Church Visuals' ProPresenter volunteer guide states the operator's core principle as "It's better to have nothing on the screen than the wrong thing on the screen" — [Church Visuals](https://churchvisuals.com/article/propresenter-training-guide-for-volunteers).

### Inferences
- Map Voxxa's modes to PSW stages: the *acquisition/analysis* stages (listen, align) can run at high automation always; the *action* stage (press key) is the only dial. "Suggest-only" = decision automation with manual action (human-in-the-loop); "autonomous" = action automation with human-on-the-loop. Rehearsal-gated per-song promotion is a per-item LOA, which PSW explicitly permits ("depending on the context").
- Zhang et al. imply a confidence display is worth building *only if* paired with a cheap override and information the human has that the system lacks (e.g., the stage display showing "I think we are at chorus 2; next: bridge"). Confidence alone will not raise service quality.
- Honeycutt et al. warn that asking volunteers to correct the system during a live service may *reduce* their trust in it. Correction should be concentrated in rehearsal (tap-along) and kept to a single "hold / back one" action live.
- Bainbridge's vigilance finding argues against designing the live role as "watch the AI and catch its mistakes". A better split: the system auto-detects its own anomalies (alarms) and the volunteer only responds to alarms or to the worship leader. This is also why the "automatic alarm system" (watchdog, Q5) is not optional.
- Amershi G10 ("scope services when in doubt") is the direct justification for graceful degradation: when alignment confidence falls below a per-song threshold, drop from autonomous to suggest-only for that song, show it, and return only when confidence recovers.
- "Automation surprise" avoidance in this domain = predictability: announce the next intended press on the stage display a beat before acting (e.g., a progress bar filling to the predicted change), so a volunteer or the worship leader can veto. (Inference from Klein et al.'s predictability/observability and Xu's out-of-the-loop description; no paper tested this specific UI.)

### Gaps
- No arXiv paper was found that studies shared control of a *low-stakes but highly visible* live task like slide advancing; the shared-autonomy literature is robotics, driving and drones. Transfer is by analogy.
- Could not read the full text of Jalaeian et al. (the fourteen heuristics) or the Xu et al. papers; the automation-surprise quotes are from search snippets.
- Found no empirical study of "veto before action" countdown displays.

## Key Question 2: What patterns exist for interactive machine learning / active learning where the human corrects a live tracker and the system improves per item (per song), including learning from a few rehearsals?

### Takeaway
There is a direct precedent in computer accompaniment: Raphael's Music Plus One learns a soloist's timing from a series of rehearsals and uses that to predict future onsets, and the Listen/Predict/Play split is a reusable architecture for a slide follower. Modern HCI work (EvolveCaptions, 2025) shows that per-speaker adaptation from live corrections plus a few minutes of targeted recording measurably reduces errors over a handful of rounds. The active-learning literature recommends routing *only uncertain* instances to the human, offering suggested labels for intermediate ones, and treating the human oracle as fallible. Shadow-mode deployment is the standard engineering pattern for "run but do not act until proven".

### Cited Findings

**Learning from rehearsals in score following / accompaniment**
- Raphael's Music Plus One system "learns and assimilates the player's interpretation through a series of rehearsals" (ISMIR 2004) — [ISMIR 2004 paper](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2004/paper/000176.pdf); CACM "The Informatics Philharmonic": the approach adapts a predictive model to the soloist's style, "mimicking the process by which human performers learn to anticipate each other's actions through practice" — [CACM](https://cacm.acm.org/research/the-informatics-philharmonic).
- Architecture: three modules, Listen (score matching), Predict (anticipating upcoming note onsets), Play (rendering audio by phase vocoding an accompaniment recording) — [Raphael, "Music Plus One and Machine Learning", ICML 2010](https://icml.cc/imls/conferences/2010/papers/904.pdf) (PDF link returned 404 this session; architecture description from search summary and the CACM article).
- Raphael treats tempo as a continuous, time-varying quantity; a model that simultaneously tracks tempo "will make reasonable predictions of note onset times" — [Raphael, Machine Learning 2006 (Springer)](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10994-006-8415-3.pdf). Jiang & Raphael (ISMIR 2020) propose a switching state-space Kalman-filter tempo model benchmarked against the Music Plus One HMM baseline — [ISMIR 2020 poster](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~yjiang3/research/ISMIR2020-poster.pdf).
- Arzt & Widmer's real-time audio-to-audio tracker uses "simple tempo models" and notes their models "need not reflect one specific way of how to perform a piece" — [Arzt & Widmer SMC 2010](https://www.cp.jku.at/research/papers/Arzt_Widmer_SMC_2010.pdf).
- Matchmaker (2025) is an open-source real-time piano score follower with a systematic evaluation harness; OLTW with a backward-forward strategy is credited with "correcting early misalignments"; the HMM variant tends to "linger on sustained notes" ("sticky") — [arXiv 2510.10087](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.10087v1).
- Survey of score following and audio alignment (2022) — [arXiv 2205.03247](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.03247).

**Learning from live operator corrections (speech/captions)**
- EvolveCaptions (Wu & Jain, Oct 2025): hearing partners correct ASR errors as they occur; the system then generates "short, targeted clauses" for the DHH speaker to record and uses them to fine-tune the model incrementally (speaker-specific adaptation). With 12 DHH and 6 hearing participants, WER decreased across five scripted reading rounds with an average of only five minutes of additional speech recording; participants found recording and correction "effortful", and acceptability depended on expected long-term benefit — [arXiv 2510.02181](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.02181).
- IBM (AAAI 2026) separates *corrective feedback* (soliciting user corrections post-deployment) from *persistent learning* (updating the model from them) and includes an active-learning framework that minimises not only how many examples a user labels but "how difficult each example is to label"; it guides the user to incorrect predictions and suggests the most informative fields to correct — [IBM Research](https://research.ibm.com/publications/corrective-feedback-and-persistent-learning-for-information-extraction).

**Active learning: ask the human only when uncertain**
- arXiv 2306.01277 proposes routing "hard (uncertain) instances" to active learning, auto-labelling easy instances, and using "label suggestion" for intermediate instances that the human verifies; auto-labelling, human correction and human verification extract more from each human interaction than plain AL — [arXiv 2306.01277](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.01277.pdf).
- A human-in-the-loop uncertainty paper describes an operator monitoring multiple streams in parallel who may "temporarily abstain from giving a label until enough examples have been presented" — [arXiv 2008.12735](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.12735) (attribution from search summary; unverified).
- "Human-in-the-loop or AI-in-the-loop?" (Dec 2024) discusses when the human should be the controlling party versus the AI — [arXiv 2412.14232](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.14232v1).
- Active learning usually assumes an infallible oracle; in practice the labeller is human and fallible (citing Settles 2011) — stated in [arXiv 1805.08578](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.08578v1.pdf) per search summary (unverified).
- An interactive interface for collecting and learning from complex annotations — [arXiv 2403.19339](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.19339v1).

**Shadow mode / dark launch as the "learn before acting" deployment pattern**
- Shadow deployment: a candidate model "receives production traffic in parallel with the existing champion model but does not serve its predictions to end users"; teams compare outputs for accuracy, latency and drift before exposing it; a shadow regression "causes zero customer-facing incidents" — [Statsig](https://statsig.com/perspectives/shadow-deployment-comparison); [Atlan](https://atlan.com/know/shadow-deployment-for-ml-models/); [Christopher Samiullah, 2019](https://christophergs.com/machine%20learning/2019/03/30/deploying-machine-learning-applications-in-shadow-mode/).
- Where ground truth later becomes available, compare shadow and champion accuracy on the same requests; guard side-effect paths with an explicit SHADOW_MODE flag so the shadow never writes — [OneUptime 2026](https://oneuptime.com/blog/post/2026-02-17-how-to-implement-shadow-mode-testing-for-ml-models-on-google-cloud/view); [AWS SageMaker shadow tests](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/aws/new-for-amazon-sagemaker-perform-shadow-tests-to-compare-inference-performance-between-ml-model-variants).

**Amershi guidelines relevant to learning**
- G13 "Learn from user behavior", G14 "Update and adapt cautiously", G15 "Encourage granular feedback", G18 "Notify users about changes" — [Microsoft Research blog](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/guidelines-for-human-ai-interaction-design/).

### Inferences
- Voxxa's existing dry-run mode *is* shadow mode. The missing piece is the comparison harness: log predicted change times alongside the tapped (or live-observed) true change times for every rehearsal and every service, and compute the Q6 metrics automatically after each run.
- The Music Plus One pattern suggests what to learn per song from rehearsals: not only "where the words are" but the *timing habits* of this band for this song (how long the intro vamp runs, how long the pause before the bridge is, whether the last chorus repeats). A per-song tempo/transition prior that tightens with each rehearsal is directly analogous to Raphael's rehearsal-trained predictor.
- EvolveCaptions' "five minutes of extra recording" result suggests a realistic budget for per-song enrolment: one or two rehearsal passes plus tap-along, not hours.
- Apply arXiv 2306.01277's three-tier scheme to tap-along review: changes the system is confident about are auto-accepted; medium-confidence changes are shown as suggestions ("I think the change was here; confirm?"); only low-confidence or conflicting changes require the founder to place the mark by hand. This cuts tap-along labour and matches Amershi G15.
- Because the human oracle is fallible, taps should be treated as noisy labels (e.g., allow a few hundred ms of slack; detect taps that contradict the audio, such as a tap during silence) rather than as exact truth.
- Honeycutt et al. (Q1) plus EvolveCaptions' "effortful" finding argue for keeping all learning input in rehearsal and making the live service a *read-only* evaluation run except for the hold/back override.

### Gaps
- No arXiv or ISMIR paper was found on *lyrics-driven* (as opposed to score-driven) live following that learns from rehearsals; Music Plus One is the nearest analogue and is instrument-focused.
- Could not read Raphael's ICML 2010 paper; the exact rehearsal-update mechanism (what is re-estimated per rehearsal, how many rehearsals suffice) is not documented here.
- No source gives a validated per-item (per-song) "promotion" threshold from suggest-only to autonomous; see Q6 for a proposed acceptance test.

## Key Question 3: How do karaoke, teleprompter and live-captioning systems present uncertainty and let operators correct in real time?

### Takeaway
Live captioning is the richest source: respeaking workflows have long had instant-correction keys, relay systems mark text as "firm" versus provisional, and EvolveCaptions colour-codes corrected (yellow) versus uncertain (red) segments. Voice-following teleprompters mostly *hide* uncertainty and instead design a safe default: hold position when the speaker goes off-script, and offer a one-command reset. Nothing found exposes a numeric confidence to the operator during a live show.

### Cited Findings
- EvolveCaptions UI: the hearing corrector "can select a recognized word or phrase and either replace it with a proposed correction or mark it as uncertain. Corrected segments are displayed in yellow, while uncertain segments are displayed in red" — [arXiv 2510.02181 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.02181).
- Pražák et al. (Interspeech 2012) describe single-respeaker live TV captioning with "new techniques for instant correction of recognition output, punctuation mark introduction or new word addition" — [ISCA Interspeech 2012](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2012/prazak12_interspeech.html). Error correction improves respoken subtitle quality but "may increase a delay before the subtitles appear" (secondary summary; see also [Springer, Int. J. Speech Technology](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10772-008-9014-4)) — unverified wording.
- Semiautomated relay captioning patents (USPTO 11741963 and family): the captioner's display highlights recent corrections and marks text as "firm" once settled, e.g. a corrected word and the text before it "may be firm as to both the CA and the ASR engine" — [USPTO 11741963](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11741963).
- BeParrot (Arakawa, Yakura & Goto, IUI 2022): a respeaking interface with parameter adjustment and pronunciation feedback; in a 60-worker study it transcribed unclear speech 32.2% faster than a conventional post-correction interface; it does not surface recogniser uncertainty to the worker — [BeParrot IUI 2022 slides](https://rikky0611.github.io/resource/slide/beparrot_iui2022_slide.pdf).
- Teleprompters: PromptSmart's "VoiceTrack" claims that if the speaker goes off-script it "will hold your place, waiting for you to return", with fixed-speed scrolling and a remote as manual alternatives (vendor marketing) — [PromptSmart Pro, App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id894811756); [Duke DDMC 2015 writeup](https://sites.duke.edu/ddmc/2015/11/19/promptsmart-automates-teleprompters-with-voice-recognition/).
- Teleprompter.com voice scrolling: with smooth scrolling off the script "jumps" the spoken word to the top of the screen; voice commands such as "Go top" reset to the beginning (vendor documentation) — [Teleprompter.com voice scrolling](https://www.teleprompter.com/features/voice-scrolling); [FAQ](https://teleprompter.com/faq/how-to-use-voice-scrolling-with-the-teleprompter-app).
- Broadcast teleprompter: SysMedia's Speech Follower engine was selected because it "needs no training — the system responds to the voice of any speaker", unlike conventional recognition that often needs per-speaker training — [TV Technology](https://tvtechnology.com/news/speak-for-yourself).
- Karaoke-grade alignment evaluation: Lizé-Masclef, Vaglio & Moussallam (ISMIR 2021) built a user-centred, perceptual metric from a karaoke-like experiment; mir_eval implements it as a skew-normal scoring of each offset (skewness 1.122, location -0.223 s, scale 0.298 s), i.e. users tolerate slightly *late* word highlighting better than early — [mir_eval alignment.py](https://sources.debian.org/src/mir-eval/0.8.2-1/mir_eval/alignment.py).

### Inferences
- The two-state provisional/firm display from relay captioning maps cleanly to a stage-display or operator panel for Voxxa: current slide "firm" (confirmed by audio evidence), upcoming change "provisional" (predicted, colour-coded by confidence band), with any slide the system is unsure of marked in a third colour.
- The teleprompter "hold when off-script" behaviour is the right failure default for a slide follower: when the singer ad-libs or the band vamps, do nothing and keep the current slide; never scroll forward on a timer. This is the same rule as the Church Visuals "nothing rather than the wrong thing" principle (Q1).
- The karaoke perceptual metric's negative location parameter (about -0.22 s) is empirical evidence that *early* changes are perceived worse than slightly late ones, supporting a "zero early moves" acceptance criterion (Q6).
- Operator corrections in captioning are word-level and frequent; for slides the equivalent is a two-key vocabulary (back one, hold/resume), which is the only live correction that scales to a volunteer.

### Gaps
- No source describes how a voice-following teleprompter recovers after losing lock mid-speech, or how its manual override interacts with voice tracking; vendor pages are marketing material.
- No live-captioning source was found that displays a numeric or graded ASR confidence to the respeaker during broadcast; EvolveCaptions' red/yellow is human-set, not model-set.
- No karaoke-system source on real-time *operator* correction was found (karaoke is pre-aligned offline).

## Key Question 4: What training and run-book designs help volunteer church tech teams adopt new tools?

### Takeaway
Church-tech practitioner sources agree on: a one-page role guide, a pre-service checklist, a shutdown guide, hands-on practice in a no-pressure setting, veteran-led (not director-led) training, and a full dry run before every service. They also warn that volunteers are "rarely plug-and-play", that confidence takes months of shadowing, that unstructured shadowing fails, and that rotation and short monthly skill sessions reduce burnout. None of the sources covers adopting an *autonomous* tool, so that part is inference.

### Cited Findings
- Church Visuals ProPresenter guide: training is done by veteran volunteers (someone who has served consistently about three months), not the director, using a short topic list. Pre-service: power on and launch ProPresenter, close unneeded programs, compare presentation against the production sheet, confirm output on the multi-view, play every video, save. During service: in position one minute before the counter, follow the director's cues, "stay flexible if the worship leader changes something", cue the next item before it is needed. Post-service: fire Clear All. Core rule: "It's better to have nothing on the screen than the wrong thing on the screen." If anything does not match the production sheet, notify the director immediately — [Church Visuals](https://churchvisuals.com/article/propresenter-training-guide-for-volunteers).
- ChurchLeaders "Church Tech Audit Checklist" (2 Dec 2025): give every volunteer "a simple, one-page guide for their specific role, a checklist for pre-service setup, a step-by-step guide for closing down systems", clear instructions for asking for help, and practice time in the worship space; write down workflows rather than assume they are known; confirm every critical piece has a backup plan; "run a live simulation" / full dry run before each service (power everything on, run worship slides and videos, start a private livestream to confirm stability); repeat the audit periodically; use a shared group chat or scheduling tool and send rehearsal reminders — [ChurchLeaders](https://churchleaders.com/ministry-tech-leaders/2208922-church-tech-audit-checklist.html).
- Life.Church video training guide: by the end of a first session a new volunteer "should be able to run through Worship, Stage Time and Next Steps with ease on ProPresenter"; "we want to avoid overwhelming new volunteers with too many details" — [Life.Church training guide](https://help.life.church/a/852077-video-training-guide).
- Logos (Faithlife) on training presentation volunteers: hands-on does not mean throwing a new volunteer into a live service; practice "in a controlled environment — a no-pressure situation"; mix methods because people learn differently — [Logos](https://www.logos.com/grow/5-strategies-training-new-volunteers/).
- Churchfront (Jan 2026) "ProPresenter Deep Dive for Church Volunteers" and "ProPresenter Crash Course for Churches" are current practitioner training curricula — [Churchfront deep dive](https://churchfront.com/2026/01/13/propresenter-deep-dive-for-church-volunteers/); [Churchfront crash course](https://churchfront.com/?p=12808).
- Church Production: volunteers "are rarely plug-and-play", and unexpected vacancies create stress because of the training gap — [Church Production](https://www.churchproduction.com/education/recruiting-church-tech-volunteers-that-stay/).
- Christianity Today (2022): confidence with a task "usually takes months" of shadowing before a volunteer can run it alone — [Christianity Today](https://christianitytoday.com/ct/2022/july-web-only/church-volunteer-technology-av-livestream-pandemic-training.html).

- Bainbridge (Q1): operators of automated systems need *more* training for rare interventions, not less — [Bainbridge 1983](https://gwern.net/doc/sociology/technology/1983-bainbridge.pdf).

### Inferences (design patterns for a 10-minute volunteer training and run-book)
- One-page role card for "Voxxa minder" (not "slide operator"): (1) pre-service: run the health check (Q5) and read its green/amber/red result; (2) know the two live controls (back one / hold-resume) and the kill switch; (3) the rule "nothing rather than wrong" means if unsure, hold; (4) who to tell and how (the worship leader via stage display, the founder via chat). This mirrors the ChurchLeaders one-page-guide and Church Visuals topic-list formats.
- 10-minute training design, based on Life.Church's "by end of session can run X" framing: 2 min watch the system follow one rehearsed song in dry-run; 3 min practise override on a deliberately mis-cued playback; 3 min run the pre-service checklist themselves; 2 min failure drill (kill switch, manual fallback to pressing keys). End criterion: trainee can state what the stage display colours mean and demonstrate hold and kill.
- Failure drills should be scheduled, not improvised: the ChurchLeaders "live simulation" and StreamingChurch.tv "simulated live services" patterns suggest a monthly 10-minute drill in which the founder injects a fault (wrong song loaded, mic muted, presentation app not in front) and the volunteer walks the run-book.
- Veteran-led training (Church Visuals) implies the goal is to graduate at least two volunteers to "veteran" (about three months of consistent service) who can train others, so the founder is not the single point of knowledge. Bainbridge's de-skilling argument means volunteers should still occasionally run slides manually (e.g., one song per month) so the fallback skill survives.
- Incident write-up after every failure (StreamingChurch.tv) plus automatic session logs (Q5) gives the team a shared record; keep it blameless and one paragraph.

## Key Question 5: What reliability practices from live production (broadcast, theatre show control, QLab / Companion) apply: rehearsal gating, dry-run modes, redundancy, logs?

### Takeaway
Theatre show control offers concrete, battle-tested patterns: a reserved, unremappable panic key (QLab's Escape) with graded stop levels, a panic duration setting, explicit awareness that "panic" does not reset external devices, a strong preference for a single control source feeding redundant machines, "define which failure you are guarding against" before building redundancy, and pinning exact software versions after the final rehearsal. Companion is treated by cautious operators as a rehearsal tool to be switched off or frozen for the run. Church livestream practice adds private test streams, recorded fallbacks and post-incident write-ups. Rehearsal gating as a formal rule is not documented anywhere I found; it is an inference from shadow-mode deployment plus theatre practice.

### Cited Findings
**QLab (Figure 53) panic / kill-switch design (community threads; official manual not retrieved)**
- A QLab developer states "The escape key is reserved exclusively for the built-in panic, so as to remain constant for all systems" and users cannot remap it — [QLab group: Option to disable escape key](https://groups.google.com/g/qlab/c/4dKi891aVns).
- Three stop levels: *panic* fades out over the workspace panic duration then stops; *stop* halts immediately but lets tails render; *hard stop* halts everything at once; QLab 1 and 2 "just stopped everything cold" on Escape — [QLab group thread, 2026](https://groups.google.com/g/qlab/c/qBwlii1XiRU).
- Panic fade duration is a workspace setting (e.g., 2 s) — [QLab group, 2014](https://groups.google.com/g/qlab/c/3MdfSrCgdNY).
- Panic can be triggered externally by AppleScript (`panic front workspace`), OSC or MIDI; a two-press macro does panic then hard stop but can misfire while typing in text fields — [OSC command for Panic all](https://groups.google.com/g/qlab/c/e30Lue-8m6M); [Panic applescript](https://groups.google.com/g/qlab/c/yE9K3ydIQ2U).
- Escape stops QLab's own cues but does not necessarily reset external gear; a MIDI cue driving DMX kept running after Escape; a proposed "panic-proof" flag with double-Escape override has not appeared as a built-in — [strange lights after Escape](https://groups.google.com/g/qlab/c/fteF6iUa5qg); [Panic Safe cues](https://groups.google.com/g/qlab/c/3sjFvP3pYPU).
- Keyboard input goes to whichever workspace is frontmost, so Escape affects only that workspace — [QLab group](https://groups.google.com/g/qlab/c/4dKi891aVns).

**Redundancy and control-source discipline**
- "Define the failure you are guarding against" (QLab crash vs OS crash vs hardware) before designing redundancy; one approach runs the same bundled workspace on both machines without connecting them — [how can I do redundant step by step?](https://groups.google.com/g/qlab/c/L-VVXh7UcWw).
- Recommend a single USB/MIDI trigger box feeding both Macs as the only source of control during the show — [Best Practice for QLab Redundant system](https://groups.google.com/g/qlab/c/oKHcw1e7BCA); Stream Deck presses hitting both machines, or Companion on a Raspberry Pi sending OSC to main and backup (untested by the author) — [Gareth Fry, Stream Deck and QLab](https://www.garethfry.co.uk/qlab-stream-deck).
- Restarting a secondary QLab to pick up edits can interrupt a live setup — [same thread](https://groups.google.com/g/qlab/c/oKHcw1e7BCA).

**Bitfocus Companion**
- One operator used Companion during final rehearsals to adjust cue volumes, then turned it off for the run; Companion ships stable and beta releases often and its QLab module is third-party maintained, so the thread urges testing the exact QLab + Companion + module versions before a show — [Problems with Companion and QLab 5](https://groups.google.com/g/qlab/c/gtyaChD_cgc).
- Bitfocus markets Companion's enterprise tier on "high availability, high security and high usability for complex show control" (vendor press release) — [ProductionHUB](https://www.productionhub.com/press/87348/bitfocus-delivers-high-availability-high-security-and-high-usability-for-complex-show-control).

**Church livestream reliability practice**
- Full dry run before each service, private livestream to confirm stability, every critical piece has a backup plan — [ChurchLeaders audit checklist](https://churchleaders.com/ministry-tech-leaders/2208922-church-tech-audit-checklist.html).
- Failure plan: pivot platform, recorded fallback ready, record locally, "write down what happened and what fixed it", review with team — [StreamingChurch.tv](https://blog.streamingchurch.tv/when-the-stream-goes-dark-10-things-to-do-when-your-church-livestream-fails/).
- Wired over wireless, minimum upload speed, pre-checks as dress rehearsal — [Resi troubleshooting](https://resi.io/blog/how-to-troubleshoot-common-church-live-streaming-issues/); [Resi failover](https://resi.io/blog/best-practices-for-establishing-backup-systems-and-failover-strategies-in-church-live-streaming/).

**Software/ML reliability patterns**
- Shadow mode: run on live input, never act, compare against ground truth when available, guard side effects behind a flag — [Statsig](https://statsig.com/perspectives/shadow-deployment-comparison); [OneUptime](https://oneuptime.com/blog/post/2026-02-17-how-to-implement-shadow-mode-testing-for-ml-models-on-google-cloud/view).
- Bainbridge: monitoring for rare abnormalities "has to be done by an automatic alarm system" — [Bainbridge 1983](https://gwern.net/doc/sociology/technology/1983-bainbridge.pdf).
- Amershi G17 "Provide global controls" and G18 "Notify users about changes" — [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/guidelines-for-human-ai-interaction-design/).

### Inferences (patterns to adopt)
- **Kill switch**: one reserved, unremappable key or hardware button (QLab's Escape model) that (a) stops all key injection immediately, (b) drops the song to suggest-only, (c) shows a red "MANUAL" state on the stage display. Give it graded levels like QLab: "hold" (pause following, keep listening) vs "panic" (stop injection for the service). Make it reachable from the stage (worship leader) as well as the booth, since Voxxa is designed to run without a booth volunteer.
- **Scope of panic**: the QLab lesson that panic does not reset external gear means Voxxa's panic must leave the presentation app in a defined state (current slide held, nothing pressed), and the run-book must say what the human does next.
- **Front-window guard**: Voxxa's existing "never press keys unless the presentation app is the front window" is the same discipline as QLab's "keyboard goes to the frontmost workspace", and should be surfaced as a health-check item (amber if the app is not in front).
- **Pin versions after final rehearsal**: freeze the Voxxa build, model files and per-song learned state at the last rehearsal; no updates between rehearsal and service (Companion/QLab version-pairing lesson; Amershi G14/G18).
- **Define the failure modes**: audio input lost, presentation app not focused or crashed, alignment confidence collapse, system clock/CPU stall, wrong setlist loaded. Each gets a watchdog signal and a defined fallback (QLab "define what you are guarding against").
- **Watchdog**: a heartbeat on audio frames and on alignment updates; if either stalls beyond a few seconds, auto-drop to suggest-only and alarm (Bainbridge's "automatic alarm system").
- **Pre-service health check** (green/amber/red, one screen): mic signal present at expected level; presentation app running and in front; today's setlist loaded and each song's rehearsal status (passed / not passed); learned-state version matches last rehearsal; kill switch test press acknowledged; log file writable.
- **Rehearsal gating**: a song runs autonomously only if its most recent rehearsal (or shadow run during a prior service) met the Q6 acceptance thresholds; otherwise it runs suggest-only. This is shadow-mode promotion applied per item, with the comparison harness supplying the evidence. No external source documents this exact rule; it is an inference.
- **Incident log**: automatic per-service log (timestamped predicted vs actual changes, confidence, every override, every watchdog event) plus a one-paragraph human write-up when anything went wrong, reviewed at the monthly skill session (StreamingChurch.tv pattern).
- **Single control source**: avoid having both a volunteer and Voxxa pressing keys at the same time without a protocol; the QLab "single trigger box" lesson suggests that in autonomous mode the human's only inputs are hold/back/panic, and any manual next-slide press automatically drops that song to suggest-only.

### Gaps
- No official QLab or Companion documentation was retrieved on redundancy or rehearsal modes; all of the above is from user-group threads (anecdotal but consistent).
- No broadcast-engineering source (SMPTE, EBU) on watchdog timers or kill switches surfaced in a church-adjacent search; a targeted broadcast search would be needed.
- No published drill template with an incident-log format was found for church tech.

## Key Question 6: Which metrics are used to evaluate live alignment systems, and what would be a sensible acceptance test?

### Takeaway
Two established metric families transfer directly. Score-following evaluation (MIREX lineage, now Matchmaker) reports alignment rate within tolerance windows (50 ms to 2 s), mean and median absolute error with errors over 2 s excluded as "unbounded tracking failures", the skewness of the signed error (early vs late), and system latency. Lyrics-to-audio evaluation (mir_eval) reports percentage correct within a 0.3 s default window, Percentage of Correct Segments (PCS, a duration-weighted overlap measure, which is exactly "fraction of time on the right slide"), and a karaoke perceptual score that penalises early more than late. "Wrong-slide seconds" is 1 minus PCS times duration; "early moves" is the negative tail of the signed error distribution.

### Cited Findings
- mir_eval `alignment.py`: `absolute_error` returns median and mean absolute error over all events; `percentage_correct` counts a timestamp as correct if it "doesn't deviate more than the window parameter from the ground truth timestamp", default window **0.3 s**; `percentage_correct_segments` builds segments between consecutive timestamps in reference and estimate and measures the overlap of matching segments as a fraction of total duration (MIREX 2020 variant divides by first-to-last reference span; the Fujihara et al. 2011 variant includes the leading and trailing segments and penalises starting early or ending late); `karaoke_perceptual_metric` from Lizé-Masclef, Vaglio & Moussallam (ISMIR 2021) scores each offset with a skew-normal density tuned on a karaoke-like user experiment (skewness 1.122, location -0.223, scale 0.298), non-symmetric, no hard threshold — [mir_eval alignment.py](https://sources.debian.org/src/mir-eval/0.8.2-1/mir_eval/alignment.py); [mir_eval docs](https://mir-evaluation.github.io/mir_eval).
- Lyrics alignment papers: mean absolute error "is affected drastically by outliers", so median and standard deviation are also reported — [Gupta et al., arXiv 1906.10369](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.10369); an end-to-end lyrics aligner reports accuracy as the fraction of words with absolute error below a tolerance τ that defaults to 1 s — [Stoller et al., arXiv 1902.06797](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1902.06797); GitHub scripts for the MIREX lyrics-to-audio challenge metrics — [GitHub topic](https://www.github.com/topics/correct-segments).
- Matchmaker (2025) real-time score following metrics: Alignment Rate within tolerance windows of 50, 100, 500, 1000 and 2000 ms, reported piece-wise and as a total over all events; Average Absolute Error with standard deviation and Median Absolute Error, in ms and in beats; "errors exceeding 2 seconds (or 2 beats) are excluded from AE calculations" to avoid distortion from "unbounded tracking failures", with coverage captured separately by AR; skewness and kurtosis of the *signed* error distribution; latency measured from detection to end of inference, split into feature time and per-frame alignment time, excluding hardware I/O. Reported examples: OLTW on (n)ASAP AAE 183.56 ms, MAE 91.18 ms, total AR 92.8% at 2 s; an HMM baseline AAE 487.73 ms, total AR 43.8%; alignment compute 0.07 ms (OLTW) vs 3.59 ms (HMM) per frame; CQT feature extraction 42.58 ms vs 0.91 ms for LSE features — [arXiv 2510.10087](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.10087v1).
- MIREX score-following evaluation origin: Cont, Schwarz, Schnell & Raphael (ISMIR 2007) define per-event error as the gap between estimated and reference alignment times, and latency as the delay between when an event occurred and when the system reported it, since "a real-time system cannot correctly detect a note until sometime after it has occurred" — [ISMIR 2007 paper](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2007/paper/000315.pdf); [HAL record](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-00839068). Unverified (from memory, full text not readable this session): the MIREX task also reports *missed notes*, *misaligned notes* (error beyond a tolerance threshold), *piece completion* (fraction of the piece followed before the tracker is lost), and mean/std offset.
- Thickstun, Brennan & Verma (2020) formalise alignment as a monotone map from score location to performance time and propose metrics compared against standard ones — [arXiv 2009.14374](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.14374).
- The 2022 survey extends MIREX metrics and computes global metrics over "correct, non-misaligned notes" — [arXiv 2205.03247](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.03247).
- Human perception: the karaoke perceptual metric's negative location (-0.22 s) encodes that listeners tolerate slightly late highlighting better than early (derived from the mir_eval parameters) — [mir_eval alignment.py](https://sources.debian.org/src/mir-eval/0.8.2-1/mir_eval/alignment.py).

### Inferences (proposed metric set and acceptance test; thresholds are my proposal, not from literature)
- **Per-change signed error** e_i = t_pressed − t_true (seconds), where t_true comes from tap-along in rehearsal or from a human-marked review of the service log. Report median AE, mean AE with outliers > 2 s excluded (Matchmaker convention), and the signed-error skewness so that "early" and "late" are visible separately.
- **Early-move count** = number of changes with e_i < −τ_early (propose τ_early = 0.3 s, the mir_eval default window; a stricter 0 s rule is possible if the model is biased late by design). Perceptual evidence (karaoke metric) supports weighting early errors more heavily than late ones.
- **Wrong-slide seconds** = (1 − PCS) × song duration, computed with the Fujihara/duration variant so that an early first change or a late last change is penalised. Report per song and per service.
- **Percentage correct within window**: fraction of changes with |e_i| ≤ 1.5 s (the founder's informal target); also report at 0.5 s and 1.0 s to see the distribution, mirroring Matchmaker's multi-window AR table.
- **Missed changes / extra presses**: a change in truth with no press within 2 s = missed; a press with no truth change within 2 s = spurious (both excluded from AE, counted separately, following the "unbounded failure" convention).
- **Lost-tracking / completion**: fraction of the song followed before the tracker's confidence collapsed or a human overrode (MIREX "piece completion" analogue, unverified naming).
- **Latency**: time from the true sung onset of the first word of the new section to the key press, measured in dry-run so that I/O is included, since the audience sees the whole pipeline.
- **Override count and hold time**: number of human interventions and seconds spent in hold per service; the trend over services is the practical trust-calibration signal (Akash et al., Li & Zhang).
- **Proposed per-song promotion gate (rehearsal gating)**: a song moves from suggest-only to autonomous when, over its last rehearsal pass (or shadow run), it has zero early moves beyond 0.3 s, zero spurious presses, ≥ 95% of changes within 1.5 s, PCS ≥ 0.97, and no lost-tracking event; it demotes automatically if any live service shows an early move or a spurious press. Service-level acceptance for "run without a volunteer": N consecutive services (propose N = 4) with zero early moves and zero spurious presses across all songs, wrong-slide seconds under a budget (e.g., < 10 s per service), and all watchdog checks green. These numbers are design proposals for the founder to tune; no source validates them.
- Learning-loop evaluation: track the per-song metrics across successive rehearsals; the Music Plus One / EvolveCaptions precedent predicts monotone improvement over the first few rounds, and a flat or worsening curve is a signal that the song (or the band's variability on it) needs a different treatment.

### Gaps
- No published tolerance window for *slide* or *section-level* changes exists; 0.3 s (words), 1 s (words, Stoller et al.) and 2 s (score-following failure cutoff) are the nearest anchors.
- No source reports acceptance thresholds used by any deployed live lyric follower; the thresholds above are inferences.
- The MIREX 2007 metric names (missed, misaligned, piece completion) are recorded from memory because the PDF text could not be extracted; verify against the paper before quoting.
